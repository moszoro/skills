# The David-Explains hook — the first line, reverse-engineered

The hook is beat 1 of the 7-beat structure (see `PATTERN.md`), but it does more work than
any other line, so it gets its own reference. Every one of the 8 reels opens by **cracking a
gap in the very first sentence** and refusing to close it. No intro, no "hey guys," no
warm-up. Frame one IS the claim.

Below: the 5 hook molds he actually uses (with the real opening lines), the DNA they all
share, what to never do, and how to build ours for the fear-of-verdict buyer.

---

## The 5 hook types (with the real openers from the 8 reels)

### 1. Stated-reason vs real-reason gap  — his most common
Tell the viewer the reason they *think* they do something is not the real one, and they
don't even know it. Instant self-directed curiosity: "wait, why *do* I do that?"
- *"The reason customers give for buying is rarely the real reason they bought."* (ice cream isn't about the heat.)
- *Ask a man why he bought the toolbox, he says "utility," but the real reason goes deeper than he knows.*
- *A group think the World Cup is elite distraction, "but these people miss" the real drive.*

> **Formula:** You think you do X for reason A. The real reason is B, and you can't see it.

### 2. Trap-question  — you answer in your head, then he flips you
A vivid either/or question that exposes a contradiction the viewer can't resolve on their own.
They commit to an answer silently, which hooks them into hearing why they're wrong.
- *"Would you still pay $700 for that Michelin dinner if it was served in a noisy diner?"*
- *How come $15 water at a gala feels fine, but $15 at a roadside store makes you storm out?*

> **Formula:** a concrete question where the honest answer contradicts itself.

### 3. Rule-of-three negation  — three denials, then one question
Three familiar things, three denials of their obvious purpose, then the question that reframes
all three at once. The repetition builds a rhythm the brain wants completed.
- *"Nobody buys a Rolex to tell the time, nobody buys a Birkin to carry things, nobody buys a Ferrari to get from A to B. So what are they actually buying?"*

> **Formula:** "Nobody does X for the obvious reason, nobody does Y, nobody does Z. So what is it really?"

### 4. False-binary demolition  — name both takes, kill both
State the two positions everyone argues about, then invalidate both in one line. It signals
"I'm about to tell you something smarter than the whole debate you've heard."
- *Most say a woman gets ready for the male gaze, others say the female gaze, "neither is correct, because…"*

> **Formula:** Some say A. Others say B. Neither is right, because both assume [wrong thing].

### 5. Famous thing + hidden fact  — "but did you know…"
Take an icon everyone recognizes, then drop a reversal about it. The recognition earns
attention; the hidden fact spends it.
- *The Marlboro Man, the most masculine icon in ad history, "but did you know the brand was originally built for women?"*

> **Formula:** [iconic thing everyone knows] + "but [the reversal nobody knows]."

## The DNA all 8 hooks share
1. **First sentence is the whole hook.** Zero runway. No "in this video," no throat-clearing.
2. **A concrete anchor, never an abstraction.** Rolex, ice cream, $15 water, Michelin, Marlboro. You can *see* it in a beat.
3. **A curiosity gap he refuses to close.** "The real reason goes deeper," "so what are they actually buying?" The answer is withheld, so you stay for it.
4. **A reversal turn within ~10s.** "but," "neither is correct," "but these people miss," "well, I thought so too. Until…"
5. **Then it cashes into authority.** A named book or a hard date lands right after (Rory Sutherland's *Alchemy*; "the war of 1813"; "1924, Philip Morris"). The hook earns the payoff.
6. **Second person, present tense.** It's about *you*, right now, not "a founder once."
7. **It targets a behavior the viewer already does or believes,** then makes it suddenly feel irrational. That gap is the open loop.

---

## Anti-patterns (what the hooks never do)
- **Never open with a date or a name.** "In 1975, an engineer at Kodak…" is a *story* open. David leads with the paradox and drops the story as *evidence* right after. Same material, wrong order.
- **No setup before the claim.** No "let me tell you about," no context-first. The claim comes first, context second.
- **No abstraction.** "Human psychology is fascinating" is dead on arrival. Anchor to a thing.
- **No closing the loop early.** If the first 10 seconds answer the question, there's no reason to stay.
- **No pure cliffhanger as the whole reel.** The lowest performer in the sample ended on an open question with no standalone payoff. Hook the next reel, but pay *this* one off (see `PATTERN.md`).

## Building ours (fear-of-verdict buyer)
Our dominant pain is a prospect who is *scared to find out if the idea works* (see the
positioning memory). That maps cleanest onto **type 1 (stated-reason gap)** and **type 2
(trap-question)**, because both put the viewer's own avoidance on the table before we've made
a single claim about the product.

Keep the stage-5 rule: the hook is about the **prospect** (their fear, their note-app idea),
not about us or the service. Operator/credibility talk stays late.

### Worked case — the Kodak reel, hook rewritten 3 ways
The scenario originally opened narratively ("In 1975, an engineer at Kodak built something").
That breaks the anti-pattern above. Fixed by leading with the gap, then using Kodak as proof:

- **A. Trap-question (recommended for us):** "What if the idea in your notes app already works, and that's the exact reason you won't test it?" → then Kodak as evidence.
- **B. Stated-reason gap:** "You tell yourself that idea is still a note because you're busy. You're not busy. You're scared of the answer." → then Kodak.
- **C. Paradox statement:** "The most dangerous thing that can happen to your idea isn't that it fails. It's that it works, and you look away." → then Kodak.

A wins for the fear buyer: the question forces them to admit the fear in their own head
before we say a word, which the payoff then cashes in.

## Hook-writing checklist
- [ ] First sentence names a behavior the viewer already does or believes.
- [ ] It's a paradox / trap-question / negation / false-binary / hidden-fact — one of the 5 molds.
- [ ] A concrete anchor you can picture instantly. No abstraction.
- [ ] The answer is withheld (open loop), not given in the first 10s.
- [ ] Second person, present tense.
- [ ] Authority or a hard fact lands right after, as proof.
- [ ] No date/name/setup *before* the claim (that's a story open, use it as evidence instead).
- [ ] For us: it's about the prospect's fear, not the product. Stage-5.
